Formal Self-Awareness in a Hurry.

This is a quick guided tour of Sections fifteen point three through fifteen point six:
internal provability, the reflection sequence, Level n, and the reason higher levels do not automatically include the lower ones.

Let us start with the big question.

How can an automated theorem prover say something about its own proving?

Not whether it is conscious. That is not the claim.
The question is narrower, and mathematical:
can the machine have a formal name for itself, and prove statements about that name?

Section fifteen point three introduces the internal provability predicate.

Think of it as a piece of the machine's language meaning:
the machine with this name proves the formula with that name.

So the machine can express a sentence like:
I prove the formula saying that I am an automated theorem prover.

There is an important warning here.

The symbol for provability is introduced with an intended meaning.
It is not automatically the full Gödel-style provability predicate from arithmetic.
The paper does not yet assume numerical encodings of proofs.
And it does not assume the usual derivability conditions from provability logic.

More importantly, a machine can make a claim about its own proving without that claim being correct.

It might prove:
I prove statement A.

And yet fail to prove statement A itself.

The reverse mismatch is also possible.
It may prove A, while failing to prove the sentence saying that it proves A.

So actual proving, and proved claims about proving, are two different things.

Section fifteen point four builds the reflection sequence.

The first formula, theta zero, says:
I am an automated theorem prover.

Theta one says:
I prove theta zero.

In ordinary language:
I prove that I am an automated theorem prover.

Theta two says:
I prove theta one.

So now the machine says:
I prove that I prove that I am an automated theorem prover.

And the pattern continues.
Each new formula says that the machine proves the previous formula.

A useful picture is a stack of certificates.

Certificate zero says:
I am a prover.

Certificate one says:
I issue certificate zero.

Certificate two says:
I issue certificate one.

The sequence gives the machine a vocabulary for increasingly deep self-description.
But it does not guarantee that any particular machine proves every certificate.

Section fifteen point five turns that sequence into levels.

A machine has Level one when it proves theta zero.

It has Level two when it proves theta one.

It has Level three when it proves theta two.

So remember the small index shift:
theta k corresponds to Level k plus one.

The level belongs to the machine, not to the individual formula.

The formula theta k is the formula associated with Level k plus one.

The machine's level set is simply the collection of levels it attains.

If it proves none of the reflection formulas, its level is zero.
If it reaches a greatest finite level, that number is its level.
If it reaches every finite level, its level is omega.

Now comes the surprise in Section fifteen point six.

You might expect the levels to form a staircase:
if a machine reaches Level three, surely it must also have Levels one and two.

But that conclusion does not follow from the definitions alone.

Imagine a machine whose available theory contains only this sentence:

I prove that I am an automated theorem prover.

That is the Level two formula.

But suppose the simpler sentence,
I am an automated theorem prover,
is not provable by the machine.

Then the machine has Level two, but not Level one.

There is no formal contradiction.

The machine is simply wrong about what it proves.

It proves a self-ascription of provability,
but the claim inside that self-ascription is not backed by an actual proof.

This is why the level set can have gaps.

To repair that problem, the paper introduces provability soundness.

In plain English:

whenever the machine proves,
I prove formula phi,

the machine really does prove phi.

This is a narrow reliability condition.

It does not say that every theorem of the machine is true in the outside world.

It says only that the machine's proved claims about its own provability are supported by actual proofs within the machine.

Once provability soundness is assumed, the staircase returns.

Suppose the machine has Level n plus one.

That means it proves:
I prove the Level n formula.

Provability soundness lets us remove the outer phrase, I prove.
So the machine really proves the Level n formula.

Repeat the same step, and all lower levels follow.

So the central lesson is this.

The internal provability predicate supplies the language of introspection.

The reflection sequence builds deeper and deeper self-descriptions.

The level hierarchy records which of those descriptions the machine proves.

But the hierarchy becomes cumulative only when the machine's claims about its own proving are reliable.

Four sentences to take away.

One:
the machine can formally say that a named machine proves a named formula.

Two:
the reflection sequence repeatedly applies that idea to the machine's own basic self-identification.

Three:
Level n means that the machine proves the reflection formula one step below n.

Four:
higher levels force lower levels only under an extra condition, such as provability soundness.

That is Sections fifteen point three through fifteen point six, in a hurry.
